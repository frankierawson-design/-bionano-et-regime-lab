BioNano ET Regime Lab: User Guide

Guidance for exploring simplified electron-transfer models at biological and nanoscale interfaces.

Open the software View source code Cite version 0.2.2

What is BioNano ET Regime Lab?

BioNano ET Regime Lab is an exploratory research tool for examining assumptions in simplified models of electron transfer at bio–nano interfaces.

It combines:

  • distance-dependent donor–acceptor coupling;
  • thermalising two-state population dynamics;
  • a nonadiabatic Marcus-rate benchmark;
  • conductance–capacitance charging timescales; and
  • dimensionless indicators for comparing possible regimes.

Important: This is a hypothesis-generation and experiment-design tool. It does not prove quantum coherence, electron tunnelling or a microscopic biological mechanism.

Quick-start guide

  1. Select a starting scenario. Presets provide illustrative starting values, not validated representations of every interface.
  2. Check every input and unit. Do not assume that a default value is appropriate for your system.
  3. Change one parameter at a time. This helps identify which assumption causes an output to change.
  4. Inspect the headline outputs. Compare the effective coupling, Marcus benchmark and conductance–capacitance rate.
  5. Examine the population plot. The curves represent model-state populations, not cell populations.
  6. Compare timescales. Large differences may reveal a missing process between microscopic electron transfer and the measured biological response.
  7. Export the calculation. Record the software version, inputs, units and outputs so the result can be reproduced.

Input definitions

Donor–acceptor distance, r

The separation between the modelled electron donor and acceptor. Coupling normally decreases rapidly as this distance increases.

The distance should not automatically be interpreted as nanoparticle diameter, linker contour length or electrode separation.

Version 0.2.1 warning: normally require r ≥ r0. Values below the reference distance extrapolate the exponential law beyond its intended range.

Reference distance, r0

The distance at which the reference electronic coupling H0 is defined.

Reference coupling, H0

The donor–acceptor electronic coupling at r0. It should ideally come from a calculation, experiment or cited literature source appropriate to the system.

Distance-decay coefficient, βk

Describes how rapidly donor–acceptor coupling decreases with distance.

HDA(r) = H0 exp[−βk(r−r0)/2]

The factor of one-half ensures that the squared coupling used in a rate expression follows:

|HDA(r)|² ∝ exp[−βk(r−r0)]

Always report the convention and units because published definitions can differ.

Driving free energy, ΔG°

The free-energy change associated with electron transfer. Negative values normally indicate thermodynamically favourable transfer. Marcus theory does not predict an unlimited rate increase as the driving force becomes increasingly negative.

Reorganisation energy, λ

The energy required to reorganise the donor, acceptor and surrounding environment during electron transfer.

Temperature, T

The absolute temperature used in the thermal and Marcus calculations. Enter temperature in kelvin.

Local overpotential, η

An additional electrochemical driving term at the interface. An externally applied voltage should not be treated as a molecular-scale overpotential without an appropriate physical model.

Longitudinal relaxation rate, Γ1

Controls how quickly the model populations relax towards equilibrium. It is a phenomenological parameter rather than a direct measurement of one biological process.

Dephasing rate, Γ2

Controls how quickly phase coherence between the model states is lost. A low modelled dephasing rate does not demonstrate experimentally observable quantum coherence.

Quantum capacitance, Cq

An effective measure of how electronic states accommodate charge as their electrochemical potential changes. It is not automatically equivalent to the total capacitance obtained from impedance spectroscopy.

Conductance, G

The effective conductance of the modelled interface. Together with Cq, it produces the characteristic rate:

kG/Cq = G / Cq

Output definitions

Effective coupling, HDA

The donor–acceptor coupling calculated after applying the selected distance dependence. A larger value means stronger coupling within the model; it does not establish a physical transfer pathway.

Marcus-rate benchmark

A nonadiabatic microscopic electron-transfer rate calculated from the selected coupling, driving free energy, reorganisation energy and temperature.

Connecting this rate to fluorescence, cellular charging or another biological endpoint requires an additional transduction model.

Acceptor-state population

The calculated probability associated with the acceptor state. It is not a cell population, electron count or measured transfer efficiency.

Gibbs-equilibrium population

The thermal equilibrium population predicted from the specified energy difference and temperature.

Unitary limit

The idealised evolution of an isolated two-state system without relaxation or dephasing. It is a reference calculation, not a claim that a biological interface is isolated.

Timescale indicator, ζ

A dimensionless comparison between coupling and dephasing or relaxation timescales. It is a diagnostic rather than a universal boundary between classical and quantum behaviour.

If the coupling and dephasing rate are both zero, the ratio is mathematically undefined. Version 0.2.1 may display infinity in this boundary case; do not interpret that value physically.

Conductance–capacitance rate, G/Cq

The characteristic rate for charging an effective capacitance through an effective conductance. Agreement with a Marcus rate does not prove that both values describe the same mechanism.

Interpreting the calculated regimes

Coupling-limited behaviour

The result changes strongly with distance or βk. A suitable experiment could vary linker length while controlling particle loading, orientation and local environment.

Activation-controlled behaviour

The Marcus benchmark changes strongly with ΔG°, λ or temperature. A prospective test could examine temperature and potential dependence.

Dephasing-dominated behaviour

Features of the reduced two-state trajectory disappear rapidly as Γ2 increases. Experiments must distinguish dephasing from conventional changes in conductivity, viscosity or kinetics.

Interface-charging limitation

The G/Cq rate is much slower than the microscopic Marcus benchmark. Conductance and capacitance should then be measured independently.

Missing transduction layer

A large difference between a microscopic rate and a biological response suggests that an explicit model may be needed to connect electron transfer, charge accumulation, ionic transport, membrane-potential change and the final measurement.

Recommended controls

  • field-only controls;
  • nanoparticle-only controls;
  • donor-only and acceptor-only controls;
  • redox-inactive or non-conductive particle controls;
  • matched linker-length series;
  • temperature and local-heating measurements;
  • membrane-integrity measurements;
  • direct measurement of redox products;
  • ionic-current measurements;
  • independent particle-loading measurements; and
  • held-out conditions not used to fit the model.

Reporting checklist

  1. Report the software version and DOI.
  2. Report every input value and unit.
  3. State the source of each parameter.
  4. Distinguish fitted parameters from independently specified values.
  5. Identify the experimental observable used for comparison.
  6. Report excluded or non-finite calculations.
  7. Include sensitivity or uncertainty analysis.
  8. Do not describe numerical agreement as mechanistic validation.

Verification status
